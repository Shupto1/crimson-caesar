Classification of Signals

Theory & Key Concepts
▼
📖 Source Textbooks: Simon Haykin & Barry Van Veen — Signals and Systems, 2nd Edition, Chapter 1 (Sections 1.1–1.3, pp. 1–13) / S. Poornachandra & B. Sasikala — Signals and Systems, 3rd Edition, Chapter 1 (Sections 1.1–1.2, pp. 1.1–1.14)

What is a Signal?

Simply put, a signal is a way of carrying information by changing a physical quantity over time. For example, when you speak, your vocal cords vibrate, changing air pressure over time to carry sound. In math, we write this as a function: x(t) if time is continuous (like an analog clock), or x[n] if time is discrete (like a digital clock ticking at steps).

How We Classify Signals (The Easy Version)

Continuous vs Discrete

  • Continuous-Time (CT): Defined at every single split second. E.g., speech or temperature. Written as x(t).
  • Discrete-Time (DT): Defined only at specific integer moments. E.g., closing stock prices at the end of each day. Written as x[n].

Periodic vs Aperiodic

  • Periodic: Repeats its pattern exactly after a certain time interval (called the fundamental period, T₀). E.g., a pure sine wave. Formally: x(t) = x(t + T₀).
  • Aperiodic: Never repeats its pattern. E.g., a transient noise or lightning strike.

Energy vs Power

  • Energy Signal: Has a finite, total energy (meaning it eventually dies down to 0). E.g., a single pulse. Average power is 0.
  • Power Signal: Keeps going forever with a steady average strength. E.g., a continuous sine wave. Total energy is infinite, but average power is finite.

Even vs Odd

  • Even Symmetry: Symmetric about the vertical y-axis. Replacing time \(t\) with \(-t\) does not change the signal. Mirror image: x(-t) = x(t).
  • Odd Symmetry: Symmetric about the origin. Replacing time \(t\) with \(-t\) flips the signal upside down. Equation: x(-t) = -x(t).

Even and Odd Decomposition

Any arbitrary signal \(x(t)\) can be decomposed into a sum of its even part \(x_e(t)\) and odd part \(x_o(t)\):

Even & Odd Decomposition Formulas \(x_e(t) = \frac{x(t) + x(-t)}{2}\) (Even Component)
\(x_o(t) = \frac{x(t) - x(-t)}{2}\) (Odd Component)
\(x(t) = x_e(t) + x_o(t)\) (Signal Reconstruction)
Symmetric Interval Integration Shortcuts:
\(\int_{-T}^{T} x_e(t) dt = 2 \int_{0}^{T} x_e(t) dt\)
\(\int_{-T}^{T} x_o(t) dt = 0\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(x(t)\): Original continuous-time input signal.
  • \(x(-t)\): Time-reversed (mirrored/flipped) version of the signal.
  • \(x_e(t)\): Even part (symmetric component about the vertical axis: \(x_e(t) = x_e(-t)\)).
  • \(x_o(t)\): Odd part (anti-symmetric component about the origin: \(x_o(t) = -x_o(-t)\)).
  • \(T\): Half-width boundary limit of the symmetric integration interval.

Calculating Energy and Power

To find the total **Energy (E)**, square the signal and integrate it over all time. For **Power (P)**, take the average of the squared signal over a long time window.

Energy & Power Formulas \(E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt\)
\(P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(E\): Total energy of the signal (finite for energy signals, infinite for power signals).
  • \(P\): Average power of the signal (finite for power signals, zero for energy signals).
  • \(x(t)\): Continuous-time input signal.
  • \(|x(t)|^2\): Squared magnitude of the signal (representing instantaneous power).
  • \(T\): Time window parameter (averaging window goes from \(-T\) to \(T\)).
  • \(\lim_{T \to \infty}\): Limit operator to average over an infinite time duration.

Energy and Power Relations (Proofs)

It is a fundamental property of signals that a signal cannot be both an energy signal and a power signal. In fact:

  • If a signal is a finite-energy signal (\(0 < E < \infty\)), its average power \(P = 0\).
  • If a signal is a finite-power signal (\(0 < P < \infty\)), its total energy \(E = \infty\).
1. Proof that Average Power of an Energy Signal is Zero:

By definition, an energy signal satisfies \(E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt < \infty\). The average power is:

\[P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt\]

Since the integral over the window \([-T, T]\) is always bounded by the integral over all time (which is the finite total energy \(E\)), we have:

\[P \le \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{E}{2T}\]

Since \(E\) is a finite constant, dividing it by \(2T\) as \(T \to \infty\) yields \(0\). Thus, \(P = 0\).

2. Proof that Total Energy of a Power Signal is Infinite:

A power signal satisfies \(0 < P < \infty\), where \(P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt\). The total energy is:

\[E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt = \lim_{T \to \infty} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt\]

We can rewrite this by multiplying and dividing by \(2T\):

\[E = \lim_{T \to \infty} 2T \left( \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt \right) = \lim_{T \to \infty} (2T \cdot P)\]

Since \(P\) is a finite non-zero positive constant, as \(T \to \infty\), the product \(2T \cdot P\) approaches infinity. Thus, \(E = \infty\).

Testing Periodicity for Sum of Signals

If you add two periodic signals, x(t) = x₁(t) + x₂(t), the sum is periodic **only if** the ratio of their individual periods T₁/T₂ is a **rational number** (a clean fraction of integers). If it is rational, write it in its simplest form as p/q. The overall period is then **T₀ = p · T₁ = q · T₂**.

Periodicity Example Adding \(\cos(\frac{\pi}{3}t)\) (\(T_1=6\)) and \(\sin(\frac{\pi}{5}t)\) (\(T_2=10\)):
\(\frac{T_1}{T_2} = \frac{6}{10} = \frac{3}{5}\) (Rational!) \(\implies T_0 = 5 T_1 = 3 T_2 = 30\) seconds
Fig 1.1 — Even (symmetric) and Odd (antisymmetric) signals

💡 Shupto's Exam Tricks & Cheatsheet

  • Periodicity Rule: For \(x(t) = x_1(t) + x_2(t)\), the ratio of periods \(T_1/T_2\) MUST be rational (a fraction of integers like \(3/5\)). If it is irrational (like \(\sqrt{2}\)), the sum is aperiodic!
  • Even/Odd Shortcut: Odd signals are anti-symmetric around the origin; at \(t=0\), an odd signal is always \(x(0) = 0\). If \(x(0) \neq 0\), the signal CANNOT be purely odd!
  • Energy vs Power: Signals that are bounded and decay to zero are Energy signals (\(0 < E < \infty, P = 0\)). Periodic signals that last forever are Power signals (\(0 < P < \infty, E = \infty\)). No signal can be both!

2022 Q1a
Define signal, ramp, unit step, and impulse signals. [2 Marks]
▼
Sol

Signal: A function of an independent variable (usually time) that represents a physical quantity and carries information (e.g., \(x(t)\)).

Unit Step Signal \(u(t)\): A signal that is 0 for negative time and 1 for positive time. Formally: \(u(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t \ge 0 \\\\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}\)

Ramp Signal \(r(t)\): A signal that is 0 for negative time and grows linearly with slope 1 for positive time. Formally: \(r(t) = t \cdot u(t) = \begin{cases} t, & t \ge 0 \\\\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}\)

Unit Impulse Signal \(\delta(t)\): An infinitely tall, infinitely narrow spike at \(t = 0\) with an area of exactly 1. Formally: \(\delta(t) = 0\) for \(t \neq 0\), and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t) dt = 1\)

2022 Q2c
Find the total energy of a trapezoidal signal \(x(t)\) that starts at 0 at \(t=-2\), rises to peak amplitude 2 at \(t=-1\), remains flat at 2 until \(t=1\), and then falls linearly to 0 at \(t=2\). [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

Due to the symmetry of the signal about \(t=0\), we can calculate the energy from \(t=0\) to \(t=2\) and double it: \(E = 2 \int_{0}^{2} |x(t)|^2 dt\)

Let's define \(x(t)\) for \(t \ge 0\):

  • For \(0 \le t \le 1\): \(x(t) = 2\) (flat top)
  • For \(1 \le t \le 2\): \(x(t) = 2 - 2(t-1) = 4 - 2t\) (falls linearly to 0)

Now, calculate the integrals:

  • Interval 1: \(\int_{0}^{1} (2)^2 dt = \int_{0}^{1} 4 dt = 4\)
  • Interval 2: \(\int_{1}^{2} (4-2t)^2 dt = \int_{1}^{2} (16 - 16t + 4t^2) dt = \left[ 16t - 8t^2 + \frac{4}{3}t^3 \right]_{1}^{2}\)
    \(= \left(32 - 32 + \frac{32}{3}\right) - \left(16 - 8 + \frac{4}{3}\right) = \frac{32}{3} - \frac{28}{3} = \frac{4}{3}\)

Summing the halves: \(E_{\text{half}} = 4 + \frac{4}{3} = \frac{16}{3}\) Joules.

Total Energy: \(E = 2 \cdot \frac{16}{3} = \frac{32}{3} \approx 10.67\) Joules.

2022 Q2d
Why is the power of an energy signal zero and the energy of a power signal infinite? [2 Marks]
▼
Sol

The average power is defined as \(P = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{E_T}{2T}\), where \(E_T\) is the energy in a window of length \(2T\).

  • For an Energy Signal: The total energy is finite (\(E_T \to E < \infty\) as \(T \to \infty\)). Thus, \(P = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{E}{2T} = 0\).
  • For a Power Signal: The average power is finite and non-zero (\(P > 0\)). Since energy is \(E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt = \lim_{T\to\infty} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt \approx \lim_{T\to\infty} P \cdot (2T)\). Since \(P > 0\), the limit goes to infinity (\(E = \infty\)).

Define a periodic signal. Determine if \(f(t) = 3\sin(4\pi t) + 7\cos(3\pi t)\) is periodic, and find its fundamental period. [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

Periodic Signal: A signal is periodic if it repeats its values at regular intervals. That is, there exists a positive constant \(T\) such that \(x(t) = x(t + T)\) for all \(t\). The smallest such \(T\) is the fundamental period \(T_0\).

For \(f(t) = 3\sin(4\pi t) + 7\cos(3\pi t)\):

  • Term 1: \(\omega_1 = 4\pi \implies T_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_1} = \frac{1}{2}\) seconds.
  • Term 2: \(\omega_2 = 3\pi \implies T_2 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_2} = \frac{2}{3}\) seconds.
  • Ratio: \(\frac{T_1}{T_2} = \frac{1/2}{2/3} = \frac{3}{4}\). Since \(\frac{3}{4}\) is a rational number, the composite signal is periodic.
  • Fundamental Period: \(T_0 = 4 \cdot T_1 = 3 \cdot T_2 = 2\) seconds.

2023 Q1b
Determine whether \(x(t) = t \cdot u(t)\) (unit ramp) is an energy signal, power signal, or neither. [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

Let's evaluate both energy and power for \(x(t) = t \cdot u(t)\):

  • Total Energy:
    \(E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} t^2 dt = \left[ \frac{t^3}{3} \right]_0^\infty = \infty\)
  • Average Power:
    \(P = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{0}^{T} t^2 dt = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T} \left[ \frac{t^3}{3} \right]_0^T = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{T^3}{6T} = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{T^2}{6} = \infty\)

Since both total energy \(E = \infty\) and average power \(P = \infty\), this signal is neither an energy signal nor a power signal.

Find the total energy of the exponential signal \(x(t) = e^{-at}u(t)\) for \(a > 0\). Is it an energy signal or a power signal? [Haykin §1.3]
▼
Sol

This is a classic textbook problem. Let's solve it in the easiest step-by-step way:

Step 1: Understand the signal

The signal \(x(t) = e^{-at}u(t)\) is 0 for \(t < 0\) (due to the unit step function \(u(t)\)) and decays exponentially for \(t \ge 0\).

Step 2: Calculate the Total Energy \(E\)

The formula for total energy is:

\(E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt\)

Since \(x(t) = 0\) for \(t < 0\), we change the integration limits from \(0\) to \(\infty\):

\(E = \int_{0}^{\infty} (e^{-at})^2 dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-2at} dt\)

Now, perform the basic integration (\(\int e^{kx} dx = \frac{1}{k}e^{kx}\)):

\(E = \left[ \frac{e^{-2at}}{-2a} \right]_{0}^{\infty} = -\frac{1}{2a} \left( e^{-\infty} - e^{0} \right)\)

Since \(e^{-\infty} = 0\) and \(e^{0} = 1\):

\(E = -\frac{1}{2a} (0 - 1) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2a}}\)

Step 3: Determine the signal type

  • The total energy \(E = \frac{1}{2a}\) is a finite value (since \(a > 0\)).
  • For any signal with finite energy, its average power is automatically zero: \(P = 0\).

Therefore, \(x(t)\) is an Energy Signal.

Define the following: (i) Signal, (ii) Impulse Signal, (iii) Unit Step Signal, and (iv) Ramp Signal. [2 Marks]
▼
Sol
  • Signal: A signal is a physical quantity that varies with time, space, or any other independent variable, and carries some form of information. Mathematically, it is represented as a function of time, e.g., \(x(t)\).

  • Ramp Signal (\(r(t)\)): A signal that increases linearly with time for positive time and is 0 for negative time: \[r(t) = t \cdot u(t) = \begin{cases} t, & t \ge 0 \\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}\]

Define Even and Odd Signals. Consider the signal: \[x(t) = \begin{cases} -\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right), & -T \le t \le T \\ 0, & \text{Otherwise} \end{cases}\] Is the signal \(x(t)\) an even or odd signal of time \(t\)? [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

Definitions:

  • Even Signal: A signal \(x(t)\) is even if it is symmetric about the vertical y-axis: \(x(-t) = x(t)\).
  • Odd Signal: A signal \(x(t)\) is odd if it is symmetric about the origin (anti-symmetric): \(x(-t) = -x(t)\).

Symmetry Test for the given signal:

For the non-zero range \(-T \le t \le T\), replace \(t\) with \(-t\):

\[x(-t) = -\sin\left(\frac{\pi (-t)}{T}\right) = -\sin\left(-\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)\]

Since the sine function is odd, \(\sin(-\theta) = -\sin(\theta)\):

\[x(-t) = -\left( -\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right) \right) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)\]

Now evaluate \(-x(t)\):

\[-x(t) = -\left( -\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right) \right) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)\]

Since \(x(-t) = -x(t)\) for all \(t\) (and \(0 = -0\) for \(|t| > T\)), the signal \(x(t)\) is an Odd Signal.

2024 Q2c
Define Energy Signal and Power Signal. Test whether the given signal, \(x(t) = e^{j\left(2t + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\), is an energy signal or a power signal. (Hint: \(|e^{j\theta}| = 1\)) [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

Definitions:

  • Energy Signal: A signal is classified as an energy signal if its total energy \(E\) is finite (\(0 < E < \infty\)), which implies its average power \(P\) is zero.
  • Power Signal: A signal is classified as a power signal if its average power \(P\) is finite and non-zero (\(0 < P < \infty\)), which implies its total energy \(E\) is infinite.

Testing \(x(t) = e^{j\left(2t + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\):

First, calculate the squared magnitude: \(|x(t)|^2 = \left|e^{j\left(2t + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\right|^2 = 1\) (since the magnitude of any complex exponential is always 1).

  • Calculate Total Energy \(E\): \[E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} 1 dt = [t]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = \infty\]
  • Calculate Average Power \(P\): \[P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} 1 dt = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} [2T] = \lim_{T \to \infty} (1) = 1\]

Conclusion: Since the total energy \(E = \infty\) and average power \(P = 1\) is finite and non-zero, the signal \(x(t)\) is a Power Signal.

Modifying the Independent Variable (Time)

We can manipulate how a signal changes over time by altering the independent variable t in the function x(t):

OperationMathematical FormPhysical Effect
Time Shiftingx(t − t₀)If t₀ > 0, shifts the signal to the **right** (delays it). If t₀ < 0, shifts to the **left** (advances it).
Time Reversalx(−t)Flips the signal horizontally about the vertical y-axis (like rewinding a tape).
Time Scalingx(at)If a > 1, **compresses** the signal in time (speeds it up). If 0 < a < 1, **expands** it (slows it down).

Modifying the Dependent Variable (Amplitude)

Unlike independent variable transformations (which shift or scale the time axis), transformations of the dependent variable affect the signal's vertical amplitude (how "tall" or "short" it is at any point):

OperationMathematical FormPhysical Effect
Amplitude Scalingy(t) = a · x(t)Multiplies the vertical height at every point by a factor \(a\). Amplifies if \(|a| > 1\), attenuates if \(|a| < 1\), and flips it upside down if \(a\) is negative.
Amplitude Additiony(t) = x(t) + cShifts the entire waveform vertically upwards by \(c\) units (if \(c > 0\)) or downwards (if \(c < 0\)).
Signal Multiplicationy(t) = x₁(t) · x₂(t)Multiplies two signals point-by-point (e.g., amplitude modulation in AM radio transmitter).
Differentiationy(t) = \(\frac{d}{dt}x(t)\)Yields the slope (rate of change) of the signal at every instant (e.g., capacitor current relation \(i(t) = C \frac{dv}{dt}\)).
Integrationy(t) = \(\int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau\)Accumulates the total area under the signal curve over time (e.g., capacitor voltage relation \(v(t) = \frac{1}{C} \int i(\tau) d\tau\)).

Combined Transformation Rule: x(at + b)

When you have a shift and a scale combined, like \(x(3t+2)\), the order of operations is critical. Here is the easiest way to do it:

Shift-Then-Scale Method Step 1 — Shift: Shift \(x(t)\) left by \(b\) units to get \(x(t + b)\).
Step 2 — Scale: Replace all \(t\) with \(at\) to scale, giving \(x(at + b)\).
Note: The critical endpoints shift from original values \(T\) to \(\frac{T - b}{a}\).
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(x(t)\): Original continuous-time input signal.
  • \(t\): Independent time variable.
  • \(a\): Time scaling factor (compress if \(a > 1\), expand if \(0 < a < 1\), reverse if negative).
  • \(b\): Time shift offset (delay if \(b < 0\), advance if \(b > 0\)).
  • \(T\): Endpoints or critical time coordinates in the original signal domain.

The Basic Building Blocks of Signals

Just like complex elements are made of atoms, complex signals are made of simple **elementary signals**:

1. Unit Step u(t)

Acts like a switch that turns on at time \(t = 0\) and stays on forever at a value of 1.

Unit Step Function \(u(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t \ge 0 \\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(u(t)\): Unit step function (value is 1 for non-negative time, 0 for negative time).
  • \(t\): Time variable.

2. Unit Ramp r(t)

Starts at 0 and climbs up with a constant slope of 1 for all positive time.

Unit Ramp Function \(r(t) = t u(t) = \begin{cases} t, & t \ge 0 \\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}\)
Relationship: \(r(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} u(\tau) d\tau\) and \(u(t) = \frac{dr(t)}{dt}\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(r(t)\): Unit ramp function (sloped line starting at \(t=0\)).
  • \(u(t)\): Unit step function.
  • \(t\): Upper limit of integration (current time).
  • \(\tau\): Integration dummy variable.

3. Unit Impulse δ(t) (Dirac Delta)

Representing a massive force happening in an infinitely small instant. It is a spike at \(t = 0\) that has an area of 1.

Unit Impulse & Sifting Property \(\delta(t) = 0\) for \(t \neq 0\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t) dt = 1\)
Sifting: \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) \delta(t-t_0) dt = x(t_0)\)
Scaling: \(\delta(at) = \frac{1}{|a|} \delta(t)\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(\delta(t)\): Unit impulse function (Dirac Delta).
  • \(x(t)\): Continuous time input signal.
  • \(t_0\): Time moment at which the impulse occurs.
  • \(x(t_0)\): Evaluated value of the signal at time \(t_0\).
  • \(a\): Time scaling constant.

Representing Signals Using Impulses

Any continuous-time signal x(t) can be built by adding up (integrating) an infinite number of scaled and shifted impulse spikes:

Impulse Representation \(x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) \delta(t-\tau) d\tau\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(x(t)\): Original continuous-time signal.
  • \(x(\tau)\): Value/weight of the signal at time \(\tau\).
  • \(\delta(t-\tau)\): Shifted impulse function peaking at \(t = \tau\).
  • \(\tau\): Integration variable representing shifted times.

Sol

This problem tests both the **scaling property** and the **sifting property** of the unit impulse function. Let's solve it simply step-by-step:

Step 1: Simplify the impulse argument using the Scaling Property

The scaling property states that:

\(\delta(a(t - t_0)) = \frac{1}{|a|}\delta(t - t_0)\)

Here, the impulse is \(\delta(2t - 4)\). We can factor out a 2:

\(\delta(2(t - 2)) = \frac{1}{|2|}\delta(t - 2) = \frac{1}{2}\delta(t - 2)\)

Step 2: Substitute back into the integral

Let's substitute our simplified impulse back into the integral:

\(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (t^3 + 4) \left[ \frac{1}{2}\delta(t - 2) \right] dt = \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (t^3 + 4)\delta(t - 2) dt\)

Step 3: Apply the Sifting Property

The sifting property states that integrating a function multiplied by a shifted impulse simply extracts the value of that function at the impulse shift time \(t_0\):

\(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(t)\delta(t - t_0) dt = g(t_0)\)

For our integral, the function is \(g(t) = t^3 + 4\) and the impulse spike occurs at \(t_0 = 2\). Evaluating the function at \(t = 2\):

\(g(2) = (2)^3 + 4 = 8 + 4 = 12\)

Step 4: Final calculation

Multiply by the \(\frac{1}{2}\) coefficient we factored out in Step 2:

\(\text{Result} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 12 = \mathbf{6}\)

What is a System?

A **system** is anything that takes an input signal \(x(t)\), processes it, and produces an output signal \(y(t)\). Think of a guitar amplifier: the raw string vibration is the input, the amplifier is the system, and the loud sound is the output.

  • Causality Trick: If the output \(y(t)\) at time \(t\) depends on any future time (e.g. \(x(t+1)\), \(x(2t)\) for \(t>0\), or \(x(-t)\) for \(t<0\)), the system is non-causal.

  • Stability Trick: A system is stable if every bounded input yields a bounded output. For LTI systems, this is equivalent to the impulse response being absolutely integrable (\(\int |h(t)|dt < \infty\)).

What is a Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) system? State and explain its important mathematical properties. [2022 Q3a, 3 Marks | 2023 Q3a, 2 Marks]
▼
Sol

An **LTI System** (Linear Time-Invariant System) is a system that satisfies both the **linearity** (obeys superposition and homogeneity) and **time-invariance** (its input-output behavior does not change with time) properties. It is completely characterized by its impulse response \(h(t)\).

The important mathematical properties of LTI systems are:

  1. Convolution Representation: The input-output relationship of any continuous-time LTI system is given by the convolution integral:
    \(y(t) = x(t) * h(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau\)
  2. Commutative Property: The order of convolution does not affect the output:
    \(x(t) * h(t) = h(t) * x(t)\)
  3. Associative Property: Cascaded LTI systems can be combined in any order:
    \([x(t) * h_1(t)] * h_2(t) = x(t) * [h_1(t) * h_2(t)]\)
  4. Distributive Property: Parallel LTI systems can be summed:
    \(x(t) * [h_1(t) + h_2(t)] = x(t) * h_1(t) + x(t) * h_2(t)\)
  5. Causality Condition: An LTI system is causal if and only if the output depends only on past and present inputs. This requires:
    \(h(t) = 0 \quad \text{for } t < 0\)
  6. Stability Condition: An LTI system is BIBO stable if and only if every bounded input produces a bounded output. This requires the impulse response to be absolutely integrable:
    \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |h(t)| dt < \infty\)
  7. Frequency-Domain Characterization: Convolution in the time domain corresponds to simple multiplication in the frequency domain:
    \(Y(j\omega) = H(j\omega) \cdot X(j\omega)\)

Write down the properties of systems. [2 Marks]

Sol

A system is classified based on the following six fundamental mathematical properties:

  1. Memory (Memoryless vs. Dynamic): A system is memoryless if output at time \(t\) depends only on input at that exact same time \(t\); otherwise, it is dynamic.
  2. Causality (Causal vs. Non-Causal): A system is causal if the output at any time depends only on present and/or past values of the input, not future values.
  3. Linearity (Linear vs. Non-Linear): A system is linear if it satisfies both the additivity and homogeneity properties (superposition).
  4. Time-Invariance (Time-Invariant vs. Time-Variant): A system is time-invariant if a time shift in the input signal causes the exact same time shift in the output signal.
  5. Stability (Stable vs. Unstable): A system is stable if every bounded input produces a bounded output (BIBO stability).
  6. Invertibility (Invertible vs. Non-Invertible): A system is invertible if distinct inputs produce distinct outputs, allowing the input to be recovered by an inverse system.

Definition: An LTI (Linear Time-Invariant) system is a system that satisfies both the property of linearity (superposition and homogeneity) and the property of time-invariance (its characteristics do not change over time).

Core Properties of an LTI System:

  • Commutative Property: The order of convolution does not matter: \[x(t) * h(t) = h(t) * x(t)\]
  • Distributive Property: Convolution distributes over addition (parallel systems): \[x(t) * [h_1(t) + h_2(t)] = x(t) * h_1(t) + x(t) * h_2(t)\]
  • Associative Property: Grouping does not matter (cascaded systems): \[[x(t) * h_1(t)] * h_2(t) = x(t) * [h_1(t) * h_2(t)]\]
  • Memoryless condition: An LTI system is memoryless if and only if its impulse response satisfies: \[h(t) = K \delta(t)\]
  • Causality condition: An LTI system is causal if and only if its impulse response is zero for negative time: \[h(t) = 0 \quad \text{for } t < 0\]
  • Stability condition (BIBO): An LTI system is stable if and only if its impulse response is absolutely integrable: \[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |h(t)| dt < \infty\]

Representing Systems with Differential Equations

In physical systems (like RLC circuits or car suspensions), the relationship between input \(x(t)\) and output \(y(t)\) is described by derivatives: how fast things change. We represent this as a Linear Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):

Linear ODE Form \(a_n \frac{d^ny(t)}{dt^n} + \dots + a_0 y(t) = b_m \frac{d^mx(t)}{dt^m} + \dots + b_0 x(t)\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(y(t)\): Output response of the system (representing the output variable over time).
  • \(x(t)\): Input signal driving the system.
  • \(t\): Independent variable (representing time).
  • \(\frac{d^k}{dt^k}\): \(k\)-th order derivative with respect to time (rate of change).
  • \(a_k, b_k\): Constant coefficients (system parameters determined by physical components like R, L, C, mass, damping, etc.).
  • \(n, m\): Order of the derivative of output and input respectively (highest derivative represents system order).

Two Parts of the Output Response

The total output of the system \(y(t)\) is a sum of two components:

1. Zero-Input Response (ZIR / Natural)

How the system behaves when the input is turned off (\(x(t)=0\)), driven solely by internal energy already stored (e.g., initial charge on capacitors). Calculated by finding the characteristic roots (\(\lambda\)) of the equation.

2. Zero-State Response (ZSR / Forced)

How the system responds to the external input \(x(t)\), assuming the system started completely at rest (zero initial conditions).

Total Response = ZIR + ZSR

Homogeneous Response Solutions (Natural Modes)

To find the homogeneous solution \(y_h(t)\), we set the input \(x(t) = 0\) and solve the characteristic equation. Depending on the roots, use the matching form below:

Homogeneous Solution Forms Characteristic Equation: \(a_n \lambda^n + a_{n-1} \lambda^{n-1} + \dots + a_0 = 0\)
1. Distinct Real Roots: \(y_h(t) = C_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} + C_2 e^{\lambda_2 t} + \dots\)
2. Repeated Real Roots: \(y_h(t) = (C_1 + C_2 t) e^{\lambda t}\) (for double root \(\lambda\))
3. Complex Conjugate Roots (\(\lambda = \sigma \pm j\omega_d\)): \(y_h(t) = e^{\sigma t} \left[ C_1 \cos(\omega_d t) + C_2 \sin(\omega_d t) \right]\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(y_h(t)\): Homogeneous solution (Natural response due to initial conditions/energy).
  • \(\lambda_i\): Roots of the characteristic equation (defining natural system frequencies/modes).
  • \(C_i\): Constants determined by matching the total response to the system's initial conditions.
  • \(e^{\lambda_i t}\): Natural mode of the system (exponential growth or decay).
  • \(t\): Time variable.
  • \(\sigma\): Real part of the complex root (exponential decay rate / system damping factor).
  • \(\omega_d\): Damped natural frequency of oscillation (imaginary part of the root).

Particular Response Solutions (Forced Modes)

The particular solution \(y_p(t)\) represents the steady-state tracking of the input signal. We assume a mathematical form similar to the input:

Input Signal Type \(x(t)\) Assumed Particular Solution Form \(y_p(t)\)
Constant / DC (\(x(t) = A\)) \(y_p(t) = K\)
Exponential (\(x(t) = A e^{at}\)) \(y_p(t) = K e^{at}\)
Note: If \(a\) is a characteristic root of multiplicity \(r\), assume \(y_p(t) = K t^r e^{at}\)
Sinusoidal (\(x(t) = A \cos(\omega t)\) or \(A \sin(\omega t)\)) \(y_p(t) = K_1 \cos(\omega t) + K_2 \sin(\omega t)\)
Polynomial (\(x(t) = A t^N\)) \(y_p(t) = K_N t^N + K_{N-1} t^{N-1} + \dots + K_0\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(y_p(t)\): Particular response (steady-state tracking of the forced input).
  • \(x(t)\): Input signal excitation.
  • \(A, a, \omega\): Known parameters of the input signal (amplitude, decay rate, input frequency).
  • \(K, K_i\): Undetermined coefficients solved by substituting \(y_p(t)\) back into the original ODE.
  • \(t\): Time variable.
  • \(r\): Multiplicity of root duplication (resonance scaling factor).

  • Total Response = ZIR + ZSR: Zero-Input Response is the system's response due to initial conditions alone (no input). Zero-State Response is the response due to the input signal alone (system at rest).
  • Roots & Stability: The natural modes of the system are of the form \(e^{\lambda t}\), where \(\lambda\) are characteristic roots. If all \(\text{Re}\{\lambda\} < 0\), the response decays and the system is stable.
  • Particular Solution: Match the form of the input (e.g., if \(x(t) = e^{at}\), assume \(y_p(t) = K e^{at}\)). If \(a\) matches a characteristic root, multiply by \(t\).

  3. Form the total solution: Combine them as \(y(t) = y_h(t) + y_p(t)\).

What is Convolution? (The Intuitive Explanation)

In LTI systems, we can find the output \(y(t)\) for *any* input \(x(t)\) if we know the system's **impulse response** \(h(t)\). We combine the input and impulse response using a mathematical mixing machine called **convolution**:

Convolution Integral \(y(t) = x(t) * h(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) h(t-\tau) d\tau\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(y(t)\): Continuous-time system output signal (response).
  • \(x(t)\): Continuous-time input signal.
  • \(h(t)\): System impulse response (the system's signature output to \(\delta(t)\)).
  • \(\tau\): Integration dummy variable representing the past time history.
  • \(t\): Current observation time (shifting parameter).
  • \(*\): Convolution operator.

The Graphical Steps of Convolution

To solve convolution visually, think of sliding one signal past the other:

  1. Flip: Flip the impulse response in time to get \(h(-\tau)\).
  2. Shift: Slide it by time \(t\) to get \(h(t-\tau)\).
  3. Multiply: Multiply the overlapping regions of \(x(\tau)\) and \(h(t-\tau)\).
  4. Integrate: Calculate the area under the product. This area is the output value \(y(t)\) at that specific moment \(t\).

    • Stays flat at peak height \(A_1 A_2 W_1\) over duration \(W_2 - W_1\)
    • Ramps down to 0 over duration \(W_1\)

Find the convolution \(y(t) = x(t) * h(t)\) where:
\(x(t) = u(t-1) - u(t-3)\)
\(h(t) = u(t) - u(t-2)\) [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

Both \(x(t)\) and \(h(t)\) are rectangular pulses:

  • \(x(t)\) is active on \([1, 3]\) (width 2, height 1).
  • \(h(t)\) is active on \([0, 2]\) (width 2, height 1).

We flip and slide \(h(t-\tau)\) over \(x(\tau)\). Let's solve for the overlapping intervals:

  1. No overlap (\(t < 1\)): The sliding pulse hasn't reached the stationary one. \(y(t) = 0\).
  2. Partial overlap (\(1 \le t < 3\)): The pulse enters from the left. Overlap is from \(\tau = 1\) to \(\tau = t\).
    \(y(t) = \int_{1}^{t} (1 \cdot 1) d\tau = t - 1\). (Ramps up linearly).
  3. Complete overlap / Peak overlap (\(3 \le t < 3\)): Since widths are equal, at \(t=3\), they overlap perfectly.
    \(y(3) = 2\).
  4. Leaving overlap (\(3 \le t < 5\)): The pulse leaves to the right. Overlap is from \(\tau = t-2\) to \(\tau = 3\).
    \(y(t) = \int_{t-2}^{3} 1 d\tau = 3 - (t-2) = 5 - t\). (Ramps down linearly).
  5. No overlap (\(t \ge 5\)): Pulse has completely passed. \(y(t) = 0\).

Result: A triangular wave starting at \(t=1\), peaking at 2 at \(t=3\), and returning to 0 at \(t=5\).

Find the convolution output \(y(t)\) for the input \(x(t) = u(t-1) - u(t-4)\) and the system impulse response \(h(t) = u(t) + u(t-1) - 2u(t-2)\). [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

First, analyze the two signals:

  • \(x(t)\) is a simple rectangular pulse of height 1 on the interval \([1, 4]\).
  • \(h(t)\) is a staircase pulse defined as:
    - \(h(t) = 1\) on \([0, 1)\)
    - \(h(t) = 2\) on \([1, 2)\)
    - \(h(t) = 0\) elsewhere.

Flipping and shifting \(h(t-\tau)\) over \(x(\tau)\) gives overlapping windows. We integrate \(y(t) = \int_{1}^{4} x(\tau) h(t-\tau) d\tau\):

  1. \(t < 1\): No overlap. \(y(t) = 0\).
  2. \(1 \le t < 2\): The leading edge enters. Overlap is on \([1, t]\), where \(h(t-\tau) = 1\).
    \(y(t) = \int_{1}^{t} 1 d\tau = t - 1\).
  3. \(2 \le t < 3\): Overlap is on \([1, t]\). The region \([t-2, t-1]\) has height 2, and \([t-1, t]\) has height 1.
    \(y(t) = \int_{1}^{t-1} 2 d\tau + \int_{t-1}^{t} 1 d\tau = 2(t-2) + 1 = 2t - 3\).
  4. \(3 \le t < 4\): The staircase is completely inside. Overlap is the whole staircase width \([t-2, t]\).
    \(y(t) = \int_{t-2}^{t-1} 2 d\tau + \int_{t-1}^{t} 1 d\tau = 2(1) + 1(1) = 3\).
  5. \(4 \le t < 5\): Staircase leaves. Overlap is \([t-2, 4]\). Part \([t-2, t-1]\) has height 2, and \([t-1, 4]\) has height 1.
    \(y(t) = \int_{t-2}^{t-1} 2 d\tau + \int_{t-1}^{4} 1 d\tau = 2 + (4 - t + 1) = 7 - t\).
  6. \(5 \le t < 6\): Almost left. Overlap is \([t-2, 4]\), entirely within the height 2 region.
    \(y(t) = \int_{t-2}^{4} 2 d\tau = 2(4 - t + 2) = 12 - 2t\).
  7. \(t \ge 6\): No overlap. \(y(t) = 0\).

Find the convolution \(y(t) = x(t) * h(t)\) of two rectangular pulses of different widths:
\(x(t) = u(t) - u(t-2)\) (width 2, height 1)
\(h(t) = u(t) - u(t-4)\) (width 4, height 1) [Haykin §2.2]
▼
Sol

Let's solve this using the **Trapezoid Rule** for convoluting rectangular pulses, which is the easiest and most intuitive way to solve it:

The Trapezoid Rule (Easy Method):

When you convolute two rectangular pulses of heights \(A_1, A_2\) and widths \(W_1, W_2\) (where \(W_1 < W_2\)):

  1. The output is a **trapezoid** of peak height \(A_{\text{peak}} = A_1 \cdot A_2 \cdot W_1\).
  2. Start Time: \(t_{\text{start}} = t_{\text{start1}} + t_{\text{start2}}\)
  3. Ramp-up Duration: \(W_1\) (the width of the shorter pulse).
  4. Flat-peak Duration: \(W_2 - W_1\) (the difference between the widths).
  5. Ramp-down Duration: \(W_1\) (returns to 0).
  6. End Time: \(t_{\text{end}} = t_{\text{end1}} + t_{\text{end2}} = t_{\text{start}} + W_1 + W_2\).

Applying the Rule to this problem:

  • \(x(t)\): \(A_1=1, W_1=2\). Active on \([0, 2]\).
  • \(h(t)\): \(A_2=1, W_2=4\). Active on \([0, 4]\).
  • \(W_1 = 2 < W_2 = 4\). The peak value is \(1 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 2\).
  • \(t_{\text{start}} = 0 + 0 = 0\).
  • \(t_{\text{end}} = 2 + 4 = 6\).

Interval-by-interval Output \(y(t)\):

  1. No overlap (\(t < 0\)): \(y(t) = 0\)
  2. Ramping Up (\(0 \le t < 2\)): Overlap width is \(t\).
    \(y(t) = \int_{0}^{t} (1 \cdot 1) d\tau = \mathbf{t}\). (Peaks at \(y(2) = 2\)).
  3. Flat Peak (\(2 \le t < 4\)): Shorter pulse is completely inside the longer pulse. Overlap width is constant at 2.
    \(y(t) = \mathbf{2}\).
  4. Ramping Down (\(4 \le t < 6\)): Shorter pulse is leaving. Overlap width is \(6 - t\).
    \(y(t) = \int_{t-2}^{4} 1 d\tau = 4 - (t - 2) = \mathbf{6 - t}\).
  5. No overlap (\(t \ge 6\)): \(y(t) = 0\)

📐 Graphical View of Overlap Cases:

Summary of Output:

\(y(t) = \begin{cases} t, & 0 \le t < 2 \\ 2, & 2 \le t < 4 \\ 6 - t, & 4 \le t < 6 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\)

2024 Q2a
Define the impulse response of a continuous-time system. [2 Marks]
▼
Sol

The impulse response, denoted by \(h(t)\), is the output response of a continuous-time LTI system when the input signal applied is a unit impulse function (Dirac delta function) \(\delta(t)\), assuming all initial conditions of the system are zero (initially at rest):

\[h(t) = T\{\delta(t)\} \quad \text{under zero initial conditions}\]

It completely characterizes the dynamic behavior of any linear time-invariant (LTI) system, allowing the system's output to any arbitrary input \(x(t)\) to be found via convolution: \(y(t) = x(t) * h(t)\).

2024 Q3b
Define the convolution integral. Explain the Reflect-and-Shift method for the graphical evaluation of the convolution integral. [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

1. Convolution Integral:

The convolution integral defines the mathematical relationship between the input \(x(t)\), the impulse response \(h(t)\), and the output \(y(t)\) of a continuous-time LTI system:

\[y(t) = x(t) * h(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) h(t - \tau) d\tau\]

2. Reflect-and-Shift Graphical Evaluation Method:

This method computes the integral by visually sliding one signal across the other. The step-by-step procedure is:

  1. Change Variable (Time to Space): Rewrite both signals as functions of an independent integration variable \(\tau\): \(x(\tau)\) and \(h(\tau)\).
  2. Reflect (Fold): Flip one of the signals (usually the simpler one, say \(h(\tau)\)) about the vertical axis to get \(h(-\tau)\).
  3. Shift: Shift the folded signal by \(t\) to get \(h(t-\tau)\). If \(t > 0\), it shifts to the right; if \(t < 0\), it shifts to the left.
  4. Multiply & Integrate: For a given value of \(t\), multiply \(x(\tau)\) and \(h(t-\tau)\) point-by-point, and integrate the product over the overlapping region to find \(y(t)\): \[y(t) = \int_{\text{overlap}} x(\tau) h(t-\tau) d\tau\]
  5. Repeat: Repeat steps 3 and 4 for all possible values of \(t\) (typically identifying distinct piecewise intervals where the nature of the overlap changes).

Mathematical Derivation:

The output is given by: \(y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) h(t - \tau) d\tau\).

Since \(x(\tau) = 1\) for \(-1 \le \tau \le 0\) (and 0 otherwise), the integral simplifies to: \[y(t) = \int_{-1}^{0} h(t - \tau) d\tau\]

Let \(u = t - \tau \implies d\tau = -du\). The limits transform as:

  • \(\tau = -1 \implies u = t + 1\)
  • \(\tau = 0 \implies u = t\)
Thus, the output is the running area of \(h(u)\) over a window of length 1 sliding from \(t\) to \(t+1\): \[y(t) = \int_{t}^{t+1} h(u) du\]

Recall the definition of \(h(u)\): \[h(u) = \begin{cases} u + 1, & -1 \le u < 0 \quad (\text{ramp}) \\ 1, & 0 \le u \le 1 \quad (\text{flat}) \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

We analyze this running integral across five distinct time intervals based on the position of the window \([t, t+1]\):

  1. Interval 1: \(t < -2\) (No Overlap)
    The sliding window is entirely to the left of the support of \(h(u)\) (\(t+1 < -1\)). \[y(t) = 0\]
  2. Interval 2: \(-2 \le t < -1\) (Entering the Ramp)
    The window enters the ramp part. The integration range is \([-1, t+1]\): \[y(t) = \int_{-1}^{t+1} (u+1) du = \left[ \frac{(u+1)^2}{2} \right]_{-1}^{t+1} = \frac{1}{2}(t+2)^2\]
    • At \(t = -2\): \(y(-2) = 0\)
    • At \(t = -1\): \(y(-1) = 0.5\)
  3. Interval 3: \(-1 \le t < 0\) (Spanning Ramp and Flat Part)
    The window covers the remainder of the ramp from \(t\) to 0, and the flat part from 0 to \(t+1\): \[y(t) = \int_{t}^{0} (u+1) du + \int_{0}^{t+1} 1 du = \left[ \frac{(u+1)^2}{2} \right]_{t}^{0} + [u]_{0}^{t+1}\] \[y(t) = \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{(t+1)^2}{2} \right) + (t+1) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{t^2+2t+1}{2} + t + 1 = 1 - \frac{1}{2}t^2\]
    • At \(t = -1\): \(y(-1) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5\)
    • At \(t = 0\): \(y(0) = 1\)
  4. Interval 4: \(0 \le t < 1\) (Leaving the Flat Part)
    The window spans the flat part from \(t\) to 1, and the zero region from 1 to \(t+1\): \[y(t) = \int_{t}^{1} 1 du + \int_{1}^{t+1} 0 du = [u]_{t}^{1} = 1 - t\]
    • At \(t = 0\): \(y(0) = 1\)
    • At \(t = 1\): \(y(1) = 0\)
  5. Interval 5: \(t \ge 1\) (No Overlap)
    The sliding window is entirely to the right of \(h(u)\) (\(t \ge 1\)). \[y(t) = 0\]

What is State-Space?

Instead of solving high-order differential equations directly, we break them down into a set of simple, first-order differential equations. The "state" of a system at any time \(t\) is represented by **state variables** (like the position and velocity of a pendulum, or current through an inductor and voltage across a capacitor). We write these as a matrix:

State-Space Equations \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(t)\) (State Equation)
\(\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t)\) (Output Equation)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(\mathbf{x}(t)\): State vector (represents the internal dynamic states of the system).
  • \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\): Derivative of state vector with respect to time (rate of change of states).
  • \(\mathbf{u}(t)\): Input vector (contains independent input source functions).
  • \(\mathbf{y}(t)\): Output vector (contains the system output functions of interest).
  • \(\mathbf{A}\): System matrix (defines the internal couplings and dynamics of the states).
  • \(\mathbf{B}\): Input matrix (couples input signals to state variables).
  • \(\mathbf{C}\): Output matrix (relates states to output variables).
  • \(\mathbf{D}\): Direct transmission matrix (feedforward gain from input directly to output).

Solution of State Equations

Solving the matrix differential equations gives the state vector trajectory and output response over time:

State Vector & Output Solution \(\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}(0) + \int_{0}^{t} e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(\tau) d\tau\)
Where:
Zero-Input Response (ZIR): \(\mathbf{x}_{zi}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} \mathbf{x}(0)\)
Zero-State Response (ZSR): \(\mathbf{x}_{zs}(t) = \int_{0}^{t} e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(\tau) d\tau\)
State Transition Matrix (STM) via Laplace:
\(\mathbf{\Phi}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{ (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \right\}\)
System Transfer Function:
\(H(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(\mathbf{x}(t)\): State vector at time \(t\).
  • \(\mathbf{x}(0)\): Initial state vector at \(t=0\).
  • \(e^{\mathbf{A}t}\) or \(\mathbf{\Phi}(t)\): State Transition Matrix (STM) (propagates initial state to time \(t\)).
  • \(\mathbf{u}(\tau)\): Input signal evaluated at dummy integration variable \(\tau\).
  • \(\int_{0}^{t} \dots d\tau\): Convolution integral mapping the history of system input to states.
  • \(s\): Complex frequency variable.
  • \(\mathbf{I}\): Identity matrix.
  • \((s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\): Resolvent matrix in the s-domain.
  • \(H(s)\): Transfer function of the system.

💡 Shupto's Exam Tricks & Cheatsheet

  • State Variable Selection: For electrical circuits, choose capacitor voltages \(v_C(t)\) and inductor currents \(i_L(t)\) as state variables. For mechanical systems, choose displacement \(x(t)\) and velocity \(v(t)\).
  • Order of System: The number of state variables equals the number of independent energy-storage elements (L and C in circuits; mass and spring in mechanical systems).
  • Output Equation: The output equation \(y(t) = C x(t) + D u(t)\) is purely algebraic and contains no derivatives.

Derivation of Transfer Function from State-Space Equations

The transfer function \(H(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}\) represents the s-domain ratio of output to input under zero initial conditions. We derive this expression from the state space equations using the Laplace transform:

Derivation Steps
  • Start with the State Equation: \[\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(t)\]
  • Take the Laplace transform on both sides (assuming zero initial conditions, \(\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{0}\)): \[s \mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s)\]
  • Move the state term to the left-hand side and factor out \(\mathbf{X}(s)\): \[s \mathbf{X}(s) - \mathbf{A} \mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s) \implies (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s)\] where \(\mathbf{I}\) is the identity matrix of matching size.
  • Solve for the state vector \(\mathbf{X}(s)\) by pre-multiplying by the inverse matrix \((s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\): \[\mathbf{X}(s) = (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s)\]
  • Take the Laplace transform of the Output Equation: \[\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t) \implies \mathbf{Y}(s) = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{U}(s)\]
  • Substitute the expression for \(\mathbf{X}(s)\) into the Output Equation: \[\mathbf{Y}(s) = \mathbf{C} \left[ (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s) \right] + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{U}(s)\]
  • Factor out the input \(\mathbf{U}(s)\) to obtain the Transfer Function \(H(s)\): \[\mathbf{Y}(s) = \left[ \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D} \right] \mathbf{U}(s) \implies H(s) = \frac{\mathbf{Y}(s)}{\mathbf{U}(s)} = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}\]

For a series circuit with resistor \(R\), inductor \(L\), capacitor \(C\) and voltage source \(u(t)\):

  • Voltage loop equation (KVL): \(L \frac{di_L(t)}{dt} + R i_L(t) + v_C(t) = u(t)\)
  • Capacitor current relation: \(C \frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} = i_L(t)\)

Rearranging for first derivatives:

  • \(\frac{dv_C(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{C} i_L(t)\)
  • \(\frac{di_L(t)}{dt} = -\frac{1}{L} v_C(t) - \frac{R}{L} i_L(t) + \frac{1}{L} u(t)\)

In matrix form:

\(\frac{d}{dt} \begin{bmatrix} v_C(t) \\ i_L(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{C} \\ -\frac{1}{L} & -\frac{R}{L} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_C(t) \\ i_L(t) \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \frac{1}{L} \end{bmatrix} u(t)\)
Component Legend:
  • \(v_C(t)\): Capacitor voltage (state variable 1).
  • \(i_L(t)\): Inductor current (state variable 2).
  • \(u(t)\): Input voltage source signal.
  • \(R, L, C\): Resistance, Inductance, and Capacitance circuit parameters.

Derive the expression of system transfer function \(H(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}\) from the state-space equations. [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

Derivation:

A continuous-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system is represented in state space by two equations:

  1. State Equation: \[\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(t) \quad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}\]
  2. Output Equation: \[\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{u}(t) \quad \text{--- (Eq. 2)}\]

Where \(\mathbf{x}(t)\) is the state vector, \(\mathbf{u}(t)\) is the input vector, \(\mathbf{y}(t)\) is the output vector, and \(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{D}\) are matrices of appropriate dimensions.

To find the transfer function \(H(s)\), we take the Laplace transform of both equations under **zero initial conditions** (\(\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{0}\mathbb{v}\) or simply \(\mathbf{0}\)):

Step 1: Laplace transform of the State Equation (Eq. 1)

\[\mathcal{L}\{\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\} = \mathcal{L}\{\mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}(t)\}\] \[s \mathbf{X}(s) - \mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Applying the zero initial condition \(\mathbf{x}(0) = \mathbf{0}\):

\[s \mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Step 2: Solve for the state vector \(\mathbf{X}(s)\)

Rearrange terms to group \(\mathbf{X}(s)\) on the left-hand side:

\[s \mathbf{X}(s) - \mathbf{A} \mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Factor out \(\mathbf{X}(s)\) by inserting the identity matrix \(\mathbf{I}\) of matching dimension:

\[(s \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) \mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Pre-multiply both sides by the inverse matrix \((s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\) (assuming it exists):

\[\mathbf{X}(s) = (s \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s) \quad \text{--- (Eq. 3)}\]

Step 3: Laplace transform of the Output Equation (Eq. 2) and Substitution

\[\mathbf{Y}(s) = \mathbf{C} \mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Substitute Eq. 3 for \(\mathbf{X}(s)\) into the Output Equation:

\[\mathbf{Y}(s) = \mathbf{C} \left[ (s \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{U}(s) \right] + \mathbf{D} \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Factor out the input vector \(\mathbf{U}(s)\) on the right:

\[\mathbf{Y}(s) = \left[ \mathbf{C} (s \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D} \right] \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Step 4: Define Transfer Function \(H(s)\)

The transfer function \(H(s)\) relates the output \(\mathbf{Y}(s)\) to the input \(\mathbf{U}(s)\) under zero initial conditions:

\[\mathbf{Y}(s) = H(s) \mathbf{U}(s)\]

Therefore, the system transfer function is:

\[\mathbf{H(s) = \mathbf{C} (s \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}}\]

The Magic of Fourier: Breaking Down Waves

Joseph Fourier discovered that **any periodic signal** (no matter how jagged, like a square wave) is just a combination of simple sine and cosine waves added together. The **Fourier Series** decomposes a periodic signal into its fundamental frequency component and harmonically related components (harmonics).

Exponential Fourier Series (Fitted for Math)

Exponential Fourier Series Pair \(x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{j n \omega_0 t}\) (Synthesis)
\(c_n = \frac{1}{T_0} \int_{T_0} x(t) e^{-j n \omega_0 t} dt\) (Analysis)
where \(\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T_0}\) is the fundamental frequency.
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(x(t)\): Continuous-time periodic signal being represented.
  • \(c_n\): Complex Fourier Series coefficients (represents amplitude and phase of the \(n\)-th harmonic).
  • \(e^{j n \omega_0 t}\): Complex exponential basis functions.
  • \(n\): Harmonic index/order (integer from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\)).
  • \(T_0\): Fundamental period of the signal.
  • \(\omega_0\): Fundamental angular frequency (\(2\pi / T_0\)).
  • \(j\): Imaginary unit (\(\sqrt{-1}\)).

Coefficient Relations

Trigonometric coefficients (\(a_n, b_n\)) and Exponential coefficients (\(c_n\)) are related as follows:

  • \(c_0 = a_0\)
  • \(c_n = \frac{a_n - j b_n}{2}\) and \(c_{-n} = \frac{a_n + j b_n}{2} = c_n^*\)
  • \(a_n = c_n + c_{-n} = 2 \text{Re}\{c_n\}\)
  • \(b_n = j(c_n - c_{-n}) = -2 \text{Im}\{c_n\}\)

Dirichlet Conditions (When does it exist?)

A signal \(x(t)\) can be written as a Fourier Series if it meets three simple rules (Dirichlet conditions):

  1. It is **absolutely integrable** over one period: \(\int_{T_0} |x(t)| dt < \infty\).
  2. It has a **finite number of peaks and valleys** in any one period.
  3. It has a **finite number of jump discontinuities** in any one period, and they aren't infinite in size.

Importance of Fourier Series in Electrical Engineering

⚡ Power System Harmonics

In power systems, ideal voltage is a 50Hz/60Hz sine wave. However, non-linear loads (rectifiers, LEDs, variable speed drives) distort the current. Using Fourier Series, we decompose this distorted current into harmonics (\(n=3, 5, 7...\)). This helps in designing harmonic filters, preventing transformer overheating, and improving power quality.

📡 Communication Systems & Bandwidth

Periodic signal transmission (like clocks in digital systems) requires certain channels. Decomposing these signals into Fourier components reveals their frequency content, allowing engineers to determine the bandwidth required for transmission and design appropriate transmitters and receivers.

Advanced Symmetry Considerations

🌊 Half-Wave Symmetry (HWS)

A periodic signal \(x(t)\) has half-wave symmetry if shifting it by half a period flips its sign: \[x\left(t \pm \frac{T_0}{2}\right) = -x(t)\]

Fourier Coefficient Effects:
  • All even harmonics are zero: \(a_0 = 0\), \(a_n = 0\) and \(b_n = 0\) for even \(n\).
  • Only odd harmonics (\(n = 1, 3, 5, \dots\)) exist: \[a_n = \frac{4}{T_0} \int_{0}^{T_0/2} x(t) \cos(n\omega_0 t) dt\] \[b_n = \frac{4}{T_0} \int_{0}^{T_0/2} x(t) \sin(n\omega_0 t) dt\]

📐 Quarter-Wave Symmetry (QWS)

Occurs when a signal has both Half-Wave Symmetry and is symmetric/anti-symmetric about the quarter-period point \(t = T_0/4\).

Fourier Coefficient Effects:
  • Even Quarter-Wave: Even + HWS \(\implies b_n = 0\), and \(a_n = 0\) for even \(n\). For odd \(n\): \[a_n = \frac{8}{T_0} \int_{0}^{T_0/4} x(t) \cos(n\omega_0 t) dt\]
  • Odd Quarter-Wave: Odd + HWS \(\implies a_n = 0\), and \(b_n = 0\) for even \(n\). For odd \(n\): \[b_n = \frac{8}{T_0} \int_{0}^{T_0/4} x(t) \sin(n\omega_0 t) dt\]

💡 Shupto's Exam Tricks & Cheatsheet

  • Symmetry Shortcuts: Save time on integration!
    • Even signal (\(x(t)=x(-t)\)) \(\implies\) only cosine terms (\(b_n = 0\)).
    • Odd signal (\(x(t)=-x(-t)\)) \(\implies\) only sine terms (\(a_n = 0, a_0 = 0\)).
    • Half-Wave Symmetric (\(x(t) = -x(t - T_0/2)\)) \(\implies\) only odd harmonics (\(n=1,3,5...\)) exist. All even harmonics are 0!
  • DC Term: \(a_0\) or \(c_0\) is simply the average value of the signal over one full period.
  • Exponential coefficients: \(c_n\) and \(c_{-n}\) are related by complex conjugate: \(c_{-n} = c_n^*\).

Find the exponential Fourier series coefficients \(c_n\) of a periodic rectangular pulse train of height \(A\), width \(W = 2T_1\), and fundamental period \(T_0\). [Haykin §3.5]
▼
Sol

This is one of the most important textbook derivations in Fourier analysis. Let's solve it in the easiest step-by-step way:

Step 1: Understand the signal

Over one fundamental period \([-T_0/2, T_0/2]\), the pulse is centered at the origin:
\(x(t) = \begin{cases} A, & -T_1 \le t \le T_1 \\ 0, & \text{elsewhere in the period} \end{cases}\)

Step 2: Set up the Exponential Fourier Series formula

The coefficients \(c_n\) are given by:

\(c_n = \frac{1}{T_0} \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} x(t) e^{-j n \omega_0 t} dt\)

Substitute our signal \(x(t)\), reducing the limits of integration to where the pulse is non-zero (\([-T_1, T_1]\)):

\(c_n = \frac{1}{T_0} \int_{-T_1}^{T_1} A e^{-j n \omega_0 t} dt = \frac{A}{T_0} \int_{-T_1}^{T_1} e^{-j n \omega_0 t} dt\)

Step 3: Integrate

For \(n \neq 0\):

\(c_n = \frac{A}{T_0} \left[ \frac{e^{-j n \omega_0 t}}{-j n \omega_0} \right]_{-T_1}^{T_1} = \frac{A}{-j n \omega_0 T_0} \left( e^{-j n \omega_0 T_1} - e^{j n \omega_0 T_1} \right)\)

We know Euler's identity for sine: \(\sin(\theta) = \frac{e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}}{2j}\), which means \(e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta} = 2j\sin(\theta)\). Rearranging the negative sign:

\(c_n = \frac{A}{n \omega_0 T_0} \left( \frac{e^{j n \omega_0 T_1} - e^{-j n \omega_0 T_1}}{j} \right) = \frac{2A}{n \omega_0 T_0} \sin(n \omega_0 T_1)\)

Step 4: Express using the sinc function

Recall that \(\omega_0 T_0 = 2\pi\). Substitute this into the denominator:

\(c_n = \frac{2A}{2\pi n} \sin(n \omega_0 T_1) = \frac{A}{\pi n} \sin(n \omega_0 T_1)\)

To write this in terms of the normalized sinc function (\(\text{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}\)):

\(c_n = \frac{2 A T_1}{T_0} \frac{\sin(n \omega_0 T_1)}{n \omega_0 T_1} = \mathbf{\frac{2 A T_1}{T_0} \text{sinc}\left(\frac{n \omega_0 T_1}{\pi}\right)}\)

Step 5: Find the DC Term \(c_0\)

For \(n = 0\), evaluate the average value over one period directly:

\(c_0 = \frac{1}{T_0} \int_{-T_1}^{T_1} A dt = \mathbf{\frac{2 A T_1}{T_0}}\) (which is the pulse area divided by the period).

State the Dirichlet conditions for representation of a periodic signal by a Fourier series. [3 Marks]
▼
Sol

For a periodic signal \(x(t)\) of period \(T_0\) to be represented by a convergent Fourier Series, it must satisfy the three Dirichlet conditions:

  1. Absolute Integrability: The signal must be absolutely integrable over any single period: \[\int_{T_0} |x(t)| dt < \infty\] This guarantees that the coefficients \(c_n\) will remain finite.
  2. Finite Maxima and Minima: The signal must have a finite number of local maxima and local minima in any single period. (This rules out functions like \(\sin(1/t)\) near \(t=0\)).
  3. Finite Discontinuities: The signal must have a finite number of jump discontinuities in any single period, and all such discontinuities must be of finite magnitude.

Explain the Gibbs phenomenon and its effects on discontinuous signals. [3 Marks]
▼
Sol

Gibbs Phenomenon:

The Gibbs phenomenon describes the peculiar mathematical behavior that occurs when a discontinuous periodic signal (such as a square wave) is approximated by a finite Fourier Series. Near the jump discontinuities, the reconstructed signal exhibits high-frequency oscillations (ringing) that do not decay to zero even as the number of harmonics \(N\) approaches infinity.

Effects on Discontinuous Signals:

  • Overshoot: At the edge of a jump discontinuity, the Fourier Series reconstruction always overshoots the actual value. The height of this overshoot is a constant value of approximately \(8.9\%\) of the total jump discontinuity height, regardless of how many harmonics are added.
  • Ringing: Oscillatory ripples ripple outwards from the discontinuity point. While increasing \(N\) compresses the width of these oscillations toward the discontinuity point, the amplitude of the overshoot remains constant.
  • Physical Impact: In digital communication channels or amplifier circuits, Gibbs overshoot can cause voltage spikes that exceed maximum circuit tolerances, causing signal distortion or component damage.

Extending Fourier to Non-Periodic Signals

What if a signal doesn't repeat, like a single handclap? The **Fourier Transform (FT)** handles this by assuming the period is infinite. Instead of discrete frequency lines, we get a **continuous spectrum** of frequencies.

Fourier Transform Pair \(X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt\) (Forward / Analysis)
\(x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(j\omega) e^{j\omega t} d\omega\) (Inverse / Synthesis)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(x(t)\): Continuous-time signal (time-domain representation).
  • \(X(j\omega)\): Continuous Fourier Transform (frequency spectrum representation).
  • \(\omega\): Angular frequency variable (\(2\pi f\), in radians per second).
  • \(e^{-j\omega t}\): Complex exponential analysis basis function (conjugate phasor).
  • \(j\): Imaginary unit (\(\sqrt{-1}\)).
  • \(t\): Continuous time variable.

  • Duality Property: If \(\mathcal{F}\{x(t)\} = X(\omega)\), then \(\mathcal{F}\{X(t)\} = 2\pi x(-\omega)\). Since rect in time is sinc in frequency, sinc in time is rect in frequency!

Let \(y(t) = x_1(t) * x_2(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) x_2(t-\tau) dt\). Take the Fourier Transform:

\(Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) x_2(t-\tau) d\tau \right] e^{-j\omega t} dt\)

Change the order of integration:

\(Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_2(t-\tau) e^{-j\omega t} dt \right] d\tau\)

Let \(u = t - \tau \implies dt = du\), and \(t = u + \tau\):

\(Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_2(u) e^{-j\omega(u+\tau)} du \right] d\tau\)

\(Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) e^{-j\omega \tau} \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_2(u) e^{-j\omega u} du \right] d\tau\)

The inner integral is \(X_2(\omega)\), which is independent of \(\tau\). Thus:

\(Y(\omega) = X_2(\omega) \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) e^{-j\omega \tau} d\tau = X_2(\omega) X_1(\omega)\).

Hence proved: \(y(t) \longleftrightarrow X_1(\omega) \cdot X_2(\omega)\).

The Fourier Transform formula is:

\(X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt\)

Split the integral into two parts (negative time and positive time):

\(X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{at} e^{-j\omega t} dt + \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-at} e^{-j\omega t} dt\)

Step 3: Integrate each part

  • Part 1 (Negative Time):
    \(\int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{(a - j\omega)t} dt = \left[ \frac{e^{(a - j\omega)t}}{a - j\omega} \right]_{-\infty}^{0} = \frac{e^{0} - e^{-\infty}}{a - j\omega} = \frac{1 - 0}{a - j\omega} = \frac{1}{a - j\omega}\)
  • Part 2 (Positive Time):
    \(\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(a + j\omega)t} dt = \left[ \frac{e^{-(a + j\omega)t}}{-(a + j\omega)} \right]_{0}^{\infty} = \frac{e^{-\infty} - e^{0}}{-(a + j\omega)} = \frac{0 - 1}{-(a + j\omega)} = \frac{1}{a + j\omega}\)

Step 4: Combine the two fractions

Add the results of Part 1 and Part 2, and find a common denominator:

\(X(j\omega) = \frac{1}{a - j\omega} + \frac{1}{a + j\omega} = \frac{(a + j\omega) + (a - j\omega)}{(a - j\omega)(a + j\omega)}\)

The imaginary terms \(j\omega\) in the numerator cancel out: \((a + j\omega) + (a - j\omega) = 2a\).

The denominator is a difference of squares: \((a - j\omega)(a + j\omega) = a^2 - (j\omega)^2 = a^2 + \omega^2\) (since \(j^2 = -1\)).

Thus, we get the final result:

\(X(j\omega) = \mathbf{\frac{2a}{a^2 + \omega^2}}\)

Note: Since the signal is real and even, its Fourier transform is also real and even!

If the Fourier Transform of a signal \(x(t)\) is \(X(\omega)\), then multiplying the signal by a complex exponential in the time domain results in a shift in the frequency domain:

\[Z(\omega) = X(\omega - 10) = \frac{2\sin((\omega - 10)\pi)}{\omega - 10} = 2\pi \text{sinc}((\omega - 10)\pi)\]

State and prove the convolution property of the Fourier Transform. [3 Marks]
▼
Sol

1. Statement of the Convolution Property:

The convolution property states that convolution of two signals in the time domain corresponds to multiplication of their respective Fourier transforms in the frequency domain:

\[\mathcal{F}\left\{ x_1(t) * x_2(t) \right\} = X_1(\omega) X_2(\omega)\]

where \(x_1(t) * x_2(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) x_2(t-\tau) d\tau\) is the convolution integral.

2. Mathematical Proof:

Let \(y(t) = x_1(t) * x_2(t)\). By definition, its Fourier Transform is:

\[Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) x_2(t-\tau) d\tau \right] e^{-j\omega t} dt\]

Interchanging the order of integration, we get:

\[Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_2(t-\tau) e^{-j\omega t} dt \right] d\tau\]

Let \(\lambda = t - \tau\) in the inner integral. This implies \(t = \lambda + \tau\) and \(dt = d\lambda\). The limits of integration remain from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\):

\[Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_2(\lambda) e^{-j\omega(\lambda + \tau)} d\lambda \right] d\tau\]

Split the exponential term \(e^{-j\omega(\lambda + \tau)} = e^{-j\omega\tau} e^{-j\omega\lambda}\):

\[Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) e^{-j\omega\tau} \left[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_2(\lambda) e^{-j\omega\lambda} d\lambda \right] d\tau\]

Notice that the inner integral is exactly the definition of the Fourier Transform of \(x_2(t)\), which is \(X_2(\omega)\):

\[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_2(\lambda) e^{-j\omega\lambda} d\lambda = X_2(\omega)\]

Substituting this back into the expression:

\[Y(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) e^{-j\omega\tau} X_2(\omega) d\tau\]

Since \(X_2(\omega)\) is independent of the integration variable \(\tau\), we can pull it outside the integral:

\[Y(\omega) = X_2(\omega) \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1(\tau) e^{-j\omega\tau} d\tau\]

The remaining integral is exactly the Fourier Transform of \(x_1(t)\), which is \(X_1(\omega)\):

\[Y(\omega) = X_1(\omega) X_2(\omega)\]

Thus, \(\mathcal{F}\left\{ x_1(t) * x_2(t) \right\} = X_1(\omega) X_2(\omega)\). (Q.E.D.)

How Systems Filter Frequencies

When you feed a signal into an LTI system, the system will scale (amplify/attenuate) and delay (phase shift) each frequency component of the input. We describe this filter behavior using the **Frequency Response** \(H(\omega)\):

Frequency Response \(H(\omega) = \frac{Y(\omega)}{X(\omega)} = |H(\omega)| e^{j \angle H(\omega)}\)
\(|H(\omega)|\) = Magnitude Response (gain), \(\angle H(\omega)\) = Phase Response (shift)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(H(\omega)\): Complex frequency response function.
  • \(Y(\omega)\): Output spectrum (Fourier Transform of \(y(t)\)).
  • \(X(\omega)\): Input spectrum (Fourier Transform of \(x(t)\)).
  • \(|H(\omega)|\): Magnitude response (gain factor scaling input amplitude at frequency \(\omega\)).
  • \(\angle H(\omega)\): Phase response (phase shift angle applied to input at frequency \(\omega\)).
  • \(e^{j \angle H(\omega)}\): Complex phase factor.
  • \(\omega\): Angular frequency variable.

Example: The RC Low-Pass Filter

A simple resistor-capacitor circuit lets slow (low) frequencies pass through easily but blocks fast (high) frequencies.

RC Low-Pass Filter Formula \(H(\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}\)
\(|H(\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega RC)^2}}\) and \(\angle H(\omega) = -\arctan(\omega RC)\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(H(\omega)\): Complex transfer function of the RC filter.
  • \(R\): Resistor resistance (Ohms).
  • \(C\): Capacitor capacitance (Farads).
  • \(RC\): Time constant of the filter (seconds), cutoff frequency is \(\omega_c = \frac{1}{RC}\).
  • \(|H(\omega)|\): Magnitude filter gain at frequency \(\omega\).
  • \(\angle H(\omega)\): Filter phase shift (in radians) at frequency \(\omega\).
  • \(\omega\): Angular frequency variable.
  • \(j\): Imaginary unit (\(\sqrt{-1}\)).

💡 Shupto's Exam Tricks & Cheatsheet

  • Frequency Response \(H(\omega)\): Obtained by evaluating the s-domain transfer function \(H(s)\) on the imaginary axis (\(s = j\omega\)) if the ROC contains the imaginary axis.
  • RC Low-Pass Filter: Has transfer function \(H(\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}\). At the cutoff frequency \(\omega_c = \frac{1}{RC}\), the magnitude is \(1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707\) and the phase shift is exactly \(-45^\circ\).
  • RL High-Pass Filter: Evaluates output across inductor: \(H(\omega) = \frac{j\omega L}{R + j\omega L}\).

Why Laplace? (Analyzing Growing Signals)

Fourier Transform fails for signals that blow up to infinity (like \(e^{3t}\)). The **Laplace Transform** solves this by multiplying the signal by a decaying factor \(e^{-\sigma t}\) to stabilize it first. We convert the time variable \(t\) into a complex frequency variable **\(s = \sigma + j\omega\)**.

Laplace Transform Pairs \(X(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt \quad \text{(Bilateral Laplace Transform)}\)
\(X(s) = \int_{0^{-}}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt \quad \text{(Unilateral Laplace Transform)}\)
\(x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi j} \int_{c-j\infty}^{c+j\infty} X(s) e^{st} ds \quad \text{(Inverse Laplace Transform)}\)
Along with the equation, you MUST specify the **Region of Convergence (ROC)** (the values of \(s\) where the integral converges).
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(x(t)\): Continuous-time domain signal.
  • \(X(s)\): Complex frequency domain Laplace transform of \(x(t)\).
  • \(s\): Complex frequency variable (\(s = \sigma + j\omega\)).
  • \(\sigma\): Real part of \(s\) representing exponential decay/growth factor.
  • \(\omega\): Imaginary part of \(s\) representing angular frequency.
  • \(e^{-st}\): Complex exponential weighting kernel.
  • \(t\): Time variable.
  • \(j\): Imaginary unit (\(j = \sqrt{-1}\)).
  • \(c\): Real constant defining the line of integration within the ROC.

Solving Differential Equations using Laplace Transform

To solve linear constant-coefficient differential equations (LCCDE) with initial conditions, we use unilateral Laplace transform derivative properties to convert derivatives into algebraic terms:

Laplace Transform of Derivatives First Derivative: \(\mathcal{L}\left\{ \frac{dy(t)}{dt} \right\} = sY(s) - y(0^-)\)
Second Derivative: \(\mathcal{L}\left\{ \frac{d^2y(t)}{dt^2} \right\} = s^2Y(s) - sy(0^-) - \dot{y}(0^-)\)
\(N\)-th Derivative: \(\mathcal{L}\left\{ \frac{d^ny(t)}{dt^n} \right\} = s^nY(s) - s^{n-1}y(0^-) - s^{n-2}\dot{y}(0^-) - \dots - y^{(n-1)}(0^-)\)
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(\mathcal{L}\{\dots\}\): Laplace transform operator (converts time domain to complex frequency domain).
  • \(y(t)\): Output time-domain signal.
  • \(\frac{d^ky(t)}{dt^k}\): \(k\)-th order time derivative of the signal.
  • \(s\): Complex frequency Laplace variable (\(s = \sigma + j\omega\)).
  • \(Y(s)\): Laplace transform of the output signal (s-domain representation).
  • \(y(0^-)\): Initial value of output signal evaluated just before \(t=0\).
  • \(\dot{y}(0^-)\) or \(y'(0^-)\): Initial slope/derivative evaluated just before \(t=0\).

Step-by-Step Solving Process

Follow these steps to solve any system differential equation using Laplace transforms:

Method of Laplace Solutions
  1. Take the unilateral Laplace transform on both sides of the ODE, substituting the initial conditions (\(y(0^-), \dot{y}(0^-), \dots\)).
  2. Isolate the s-domain output variable \(Y(s)\) algebraically. This usually gives a rational fraction:
    \(Y(s) = \frac{N(s)}{D(s)}\)
  3. Decompose \(Y(s)\) using **Partial Fraction Expansion (PFE)**:
    For simple poles: \(\frac{N(s)}{(s-p_1)(s-p_2)} = \frac{K_1}{s-p_1} + \frac{K_2}{s-p_2}\)
    For repeated poles: \(\frac{N(s)}{(s-p)^2} = \frac{K_1}{s-p} + \frac{K_2}{(s-p)^2}\)
  4. Take the inverse Laplace transform \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{Y(s)\}\) for each partial term to obtain the complete time-domain response \(y(t)\).
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(Y(s)\): Total output response in the s-domain.
  • \(N(s), D(s)\): Numerator and denominator polynomials of the transfer function/output.
  • \(p_i\): System poles (roots of the characteristic polynomial \(D(s) = 0\)).
  • \(K_i\): Partial fraction coefficients (residues at each pole).
  • \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{\dots\}\): Inverse Laplace transform operator.

💡 Shupto's Exam Tricks & Cheatsheet

  • ROC Boundaries: The Region of Convergence is bounded by poles and cannot contain any poles.
  • System Stability & Causality:
    • Causal system \(\implies\) ROC is a right-half plane to the right of the rightmost pole.
    • BIBO Stable system \(\implies\) ROC must contain the imaginary axis (\(\text{Re}\{s\} = 0\)).
    • Causal + Stable \(\implies\) All poles must lie strictly in the Left Half Plane (LHP).
  • Initial and Final Value Theorems:
    • Initial Value: \(x(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} sX(s)\)
    • Final Value: \(x(\infty) = \lim_{s \to 0} sX(s)\) (only valid if all poles of \(sX(s)\) are in the LHP).

Establish the relationship between the Laplace transform and the Fourier transform. [2 Marks]
▼
Sol

The bilateral Laplace transform is defined as:

\(X(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt \quad \text{where } s = \sigma + j\omega\)

The continuous-time Fourier transform is defined as:

\(X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt\)

Key Relationships:

  1. Evaluation on the imaginary axis: If the Region of Convergence (ROC) of the Laplace transform includes the imaginary axis (\(\sigma = 0\)), then the Fourier transform is simply the Laplace transform evaluated at \(s = j\omega\):
    \(X(j\omega) = \left.X(s)\right|_{s=j\omega}\)
  2. Fourier Transform of weighted signal: Substituting \(s = \sigma + j\omega\) into the Laplace transform formula:
    \(X(\sigma + j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} [x(t)e^{-\sigma t}] e^{-j\omega t} dt = \mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\}\)
    Thus, the Laplace transform of a signal \(x(t)\) can be interpreted as the Fourier transform of the exponentially weighted signal \(x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\). The weighting factor \(e^{-\sigma t}\) forces convergence for signals that do not have a Fourier transform (e.g., growing exponentials).

▼
Sol

This is a classic and very common textbook problem. Let's solve it in the easiest step-by-step way:

Step 1: Express sine in exponential form

Using Euler's identity, we write the sine wave as a difference of two complex exponentials:

\(\sin(\omega_0 t) = \frac{e^{j\omega_0 t} - e^{-j\omega_0 t}}{2j}\)

Step 2: Set up the Laplace Transform integral

The unilateral Laplace transform is defined as:

\(X(s) = \int_{0}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt\)

Substitute our exponential form into the definition:

\(X(s) = \int_{0}^{\infty} \left[ \frac{e^{j\omega_0 t} - e^{-j\omega_0 t}}{2j} \right] e^{-st} dt = \frac{1}{2j} \left[ \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(s - j\omega_0)t} dt - \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(s + j\omega_0)t} dt \right]\)

Step 3: Integrate each term

Recall the standard transform: \(\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-at} dt = \frac{1}{a}\) (which converges for \(\text{Re}\{a\} > 0\)):

  • Term 1: \(\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(s - j\omega_0)t} dt = \frac{1}{s - j\omega_0}\) (converges for \(\text{Re}\{s - j\omega_0\} > 0 \implies \text{Re}\{s\} > 0\))
  • Term 2: \(\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(s + j\omega_0)t} dt = \frac{1}{s + j\omega_0}\) (converges for \(\text{Re}\{s + j\omega_0\} > 0 \implies \text{Re}\{s\} > 0\))

Step 4: Combine the two terms

Now, combine the fractions using a common denominator:

\(X(s) = \frac{1}{2j} \left[ \frac{1}{s - j\omega_0} - \frac{1}{s + j\omega_0} \right] = \frac{1}{2j} \left[ \frac{(s + j\omega_0) - (s - j\omega_0)}{(s - j\omega_0)(s + j\omega_0)} \right]\)

Simplify the numerator: \((s + j\omega_0) - (s - j\omega_0) = 2j\omega_0\).

Simplify the denominator: \((s - j\omega_0)(s + j\omega_0) = s^2 - (j\omega_0)^2 = s^2 + \omega_0^2\).

Multiply them together:

\(X(s) = \frac{1}{2j} \cdot \frac{2j\omega_0}{s^2 + \omega_0^2} = \mathbf{\frac{\omega_0}{s^2 + \omega_0^2}}\)

Step 5: Specify Region of Convergence (ROC)

Since the integration converges when \(\text{Re}\{s\} > 0\), the poles are on the imaginary axis at \(s = \pm j\omega_0\) (with real part 0).

Thus, the ROC is: \(\text{Re}\{s\} > 0\).

1. Definition of the Laplace Transform:

The bilateral (two-sided) Laplace Transform of a continuous-time signal \(x(t)\) is defined as:

\[X(s) = \frac{1}{s + 1} - \frac{1}{s + 2} + \frac{2}{(s + 2)^2}\]

Step 4: Find the inverse Laplace transform

Applying the standard inverse Laplace transform pairs:

  • \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s + 1}\right\} = e^{-t} u(t)\)
  • \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s + 2}\right\} = e^{-2t} u(t)\)
  • \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{(s + 2)^2}\right\} = t e^{-2t} u(t) \implies \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{2}{(s + 2)^2}\right\} = 2t e^{-2t} u(t)\)

Combining the terms, we obtain the time-domain signal:

\[x(t) = \left( e^{-t} - e^{-2t} + 2t e^{-2t} \right) u(t)\]

▼
Sol

1. Theorem Statements:

  • Initial Value Theorem (IVT): If \(x(t)\) is a signal with Laplace Transform \(X(s)\), and its derivative is Laplace transformable, the initial value \(x(0^+)\) is given by: \[x(0^+) = \lim_{t \to 0^+} x(t) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s X(s)\] Note: The theorem applies to right-sided signals that do not contain an impulse at \(t=0\).
  • Final Value Theorem (FVT): If \(x(t)\) is a stable signal with Laplace Transform \(X(s)\), and all poles of \(sX(s)\) lie in the open left-half of the \(s\)-plane (i.e., \(\text{Re}\{s\} < 0\)), then the final value \(x(\infty)\) is given by: \[x(\infty) = \lim_{t \to \infty} x(t) = \lim_{s \to 0} s X(s)\]

2. Evaluating Initial Value \(x(0^+)\) and Final Value \(x(\infty)\):

We are given: \(X(s) = e^{-2s} \frac{-2}{s(s+2)}\).

Notice the exponential term \(e^{-2s}\). By the time-shifting property of the Laplace Transform (\(\mathcal{L}\{v(t-t_0)\} = e^{-s t_0} V(s)\)), the signal has a time delay of \(t_0 = 2\) seconds. Therefore, \(x(t) = 0\) for \(t < 2\).

  • Initial Value \(x(0^+)\):
    Since the signal does not start until \(t=2\), the initial value must be \(x(0^+) = 0\).
    Using the Initial Value Theorem directly: \[x(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s X(s) = \lim_{s \to \infty} s \left( e^{-2s} \frac{-2}{s(s+2)} \right) = \lim_{s \to \infty} e^{-2s} \frac{-2}{s + 2} = 0\] This confirms the time-domain prediction.
  • Final Value \(x(\infty)\):
    Let's check the pole locations of \(s X(s)\) to ensure FVT is valid: \[s X(s) = e^{-2s} \frac{-2}{s+2}\] The pole of this expression is at \(s = -2\). Since the real part of this pole is negative (\(\text{Re}\{-2\} = -2 < 0\)), the FVT stability condition is satisfied.
    Applying the Final Value Theorem: \[x(\infty) = \lim_{s \to 0} s X(s) = \lim_{s \to 0} s \left( e^{-2s} \frac{-2}{s(s+2)} \right) = \lim_{s \to 0} e^{-2s} \frac{-2}{s + 2} = e^0 \frac{-2}{2} = -1\]

Let's find the inverse Laplace transform of \(X(s) = e^{-2s} V(s)\), where \(V(s) = \frac{-2}{s(s+2)}\):

\[V(s) = \frac{-2}{s(s+2)} = \frac{-1}{s} + \frac{1}{s+2} \implies v(t) = (-1 + e^{-2t}) u(t)\]

Applying the time shift of 2 seconds:

\[x(t) = v(t-2) = \left( -1 + e^{-2(t-2)} \right) u(t-2)\]

Evaluating this expression at \(t = 0^+\) and \(t \to \infty\):

  • \(x(0^+) = 0\) (since \(u(0-2) = 0\)).
  • \(x(\infty) = \lim_{t \to \infty} (-1 + e^{-2(t-2)}) = -1\).

This matches the theorem results perfectly!

1. Nyquist Sampling Theorem (How Analog goes Digital)

To record real-world analog sounds into a computer without losing quality, you must record samples at a speed at least **double** the highest frequency in the sound:

Nyquist Rate \(f_s \ge 2 f_{\text{max}}\)
If \(f_s < 2 f_{\text{max}}\), you get **Aliasing** (frequencies overlap and distort).
Component Legend ("What is called what"):
  • \(f_s\): Sampling frequency (number of samples taken per second, in Hertz).
  • \(f_{\text{max}}\): Maximum frequency component present in the continuous-time analog signal (highest signal bandwidth, in Hertz).
  • \(2 f_{\text{max}}\): The Nyquist rate limit (absolute minimum sampling rate to guarantee perfect reconstruction of the signal).

2. Amplitude Modulation & Demodulation (AM)

Modulation: Message signals (like voice) have low frequencies and cannot travel far. We multiply the message \(x(t)\) by a high-frequency carrier wave \(\cos(\omega_c t)\) to shift its spectrum up to high frequencies: \(y(t) = x(t)\cos(\omega_c t)\).

Demodulation: The process of recovering \(x(t)\) from the received carrier at the receiver:

  • Synchronous Demodulation: Multiply the received wave by a local carrier \(\cos(\omega_c t)\) and pass it through a Low-Pass Filter (LPF) to extract the baseband signal.
  • Envelope Detection: A simple diode rectifier and RC circuit that tracks the peaks (envelope) of the carrier amplitude.

3. Analog Electrical & Mechanical Systems (Analogies)

We can model mechanical translational systems using electrical RLC circuits by matching their differential equations (Force-Voltage and Force-Current analogies):

4. Multiplexing: FDM & TDM

Multiplexing allows multiple distinct message signals to travel over a single transmission channel simultaneously:

Frequency-Division Multiplexing (FDM)

Signals share the channel by occupying different frequency bands. Each signal is shifted to its own frequency slot using modulation. Small guard bands separate the channels to avoid frequency interference.

Time-Division Multiplexing (TDM)

Signals share the channel by occupying different time slots. Only one signal is transmitted at a time using the full bandwidth of the channel. Small guard times separate the time slots to avoid overlapping delays.

💡 Shupto's Exam Tricks & Cheatsheet

  • Nyquist Sampling Rate: Always double the maximum frequency component: \(f_s \ge 2 f_{\text{max}}\). If the signal is \(x(t) = \cos(200\pi t)\), then \(\omega = 200\pi \implies f = 100\) Hz, so \(f_s \ge 200\) Hz.
  • AM Frequency Shift: Modulating by \(\cos(\omega_c t)\) shifts the baseband spectrum \(X(\omega)\) up and down to be centered at \(\pm\omega_c\) and scales it by 1/2.
  • Multiplexing separation: FDM separates signals by **Frequency bands** (requires guard bands); TDM separates signals by **Time slots** (requires guard times).

Q1. Write down the procedure of solving a Differential Equation. Determine the homogeneous solution for the system described by the differential equation:
\(\frac{d^2}{dt^2}y(t) + 5\frac{d}{dt}y(t) + 6y(t) = 2x(t) + \frac{d}{dt}x(t)\). [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

Procedure:

  1. Find the Homogeneous Solution (\(y_h(t)\)): Set input \(x(t) = 0\), write the characteristic equation \(a_n \lambda^n + \dots + a_0 = 0\), solve for roots \(\lambda_i\), and form \(y_h(t) = \sum C_i e^{\lambda_i t}\) (with adjustments for repeated or complex roots).
  2. Find the Particular Solution (\(y_p(t)\)): Assume a form matching the input shape \(x(t)\), substitute it into the ODE, and solve for scaling constants.
  3. Form the Total Solution: Set \(y(t) = y_h(t) + y_p(t)\).
  4. Apply Initial Conditions: Use initial values (\(y(0), \dot{y}(0)\dots\)) to solve for the arbitrary homogeneous constants \(C_i\).

Homogeneous Solution calculation:

  • Homogeneous ODE: \(\frac{d^2}{dt^2}y_h(t) + 5\frac{d}{dt}y_h(t) + 6y_h(t) = 0\)
  • Characteristic Equation: \(\lambda^2 + 5\lambda + 6 = 0 \implies (\lambda+2)(\lambda+3) = 0\)
  • Characteristic Roots: \(\lambda_1 = -2\), \(\lambda_2 = -3\).
  • General Homogeneous Solution: \(y_h(t) = C_1 e^{-2t} + C_2 e^{-3t}\)

Q2. Why are Fourier Series Coefficients known as a frequency domain representation of continuous-time periodic signals? What are the Dirichlet Conditions for continuous-time periodic Fourier Series? [2 Marks]
▼
Sol

Why Frequency Domain: Because the coefficients \(c_n\) describe the exact amplitude and phase of the sinusoids at discrete harmonically related frequencies (\(n\omega_0\)) that make up the signal. They map the signal strength onto a frequency grid.

Dirichlet Conditions: For a periodic signal \(x(t)\) to be represented by a Fourier Series, it must satisfy the following three conditions:

  1. \(x(t)\) must be absolutely integrable over any period: \(\int_{T_0} |x(t)| dt < \infty\).
  2. \(x(t)\) must have a finite number of maxima and minima in any one period.
  3. \(x(t)\) must have a finite number of discontinuities in any one period, and each discontinuity must be of finite size.

Q3. Use the differentiation in time and differentiation in frequency properties to determine the Fourier Transform of the Gaussian pulse defined by \(g(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2}\). [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

Take the derivative of \(g(t)\) with respect to \(t\):

\(g'(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2} \right) = -t \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2} = -t \cdot g(t)\)

This gives the differential relation: \(g'(t) + t \cdot g(t) = 0\).

Now take the Fourier Transform of both sides:

  • By differentiation in time: \(\mathcal{F}\{g'(t)\} = j\omega G(\omega)\)
  • By differentiation in frequency: \(\mathcal{F}\{t g(t)\} = j \frac{dG(\omega)}{d\omega}\)

Substitute these into the equation:

\(j\omega G(\omega) + j \frac{dG(\omega)}{d\omega} = 0 \implies \frac{dG(\omega)}{d\omega} + \omega G(\omega) = 0\)

This is a simple separable first-order differential equation in the frequency domain:

\(\frac{dG(\omega)}{G(\omega)} = -\omega d\omega \implies \ln G(\omega) = -\frac{\omega^2}{2} + C \implies G(\omega) = A e^{-\omega^2/2}\)

To find \(A\), evaluate the integral at \(\omega = 0\):

\(G(0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-t^2/2} dt = 1\) (since it's a standard normal distribution probability curve). Thus, \(A = 1\).

Final Solution: \(G(\omega) = e^{-\omega^2/2}\) (The Gaussian pulse is its own Fourier Transform!).

Q3. State the frequency-shifting property of the Fourier representation. Use it to determine the FT of the complex sinusoidal pulse \(z(t) = e^{j10t}\) for \(|t| < \pi\), and \(0\) for \(|t| > \pi\). [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

Frequency Shifting Property: If \(\mathcal{F}\{x(t)\} = X(\omega)\), then \(\mathcal{F}\{e^{j\omega_0 t}x(t)\} = X(\omega - \omega_0)\).

Finding FT of \(z(t)\):

  • We can write \(z(t) = e^{j10t} \cdot p(t)\), where \(p(t)\) is a rectangular pulse of width \(2\pi\) centered at 0:
    \(p(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & |t| < \pi \\ 0, & |t| > \pi \end{cases}\)
  • The FT of this rectangular pulse is:
    \(P(\omega) = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1 \cdot e^{-j\omega t} dt = \frac{e^{j\omega\pi} - e^{-j\omega\pi}}{j\omega} = \frac{2\sin(\pi\omega)}{\omega}\)
  • Apply the frequency-shifting property with \(\omega_0 = 10\):
    \(Z(\omega) = P(\omega - 10) = \frac{2\sin(\pi(\omega-10))}{\omega - 10}\)

Q1. Define Unit Impulse Signal & Unit Step Signal and write down the significance of these signals. [2 Marks]
▼
Sol

Definitions:

  • Unit Step Signal (\(u(t)\)): A signal that is 0 for negative time and 1 for positive time:
    \(u(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t \ge 0 \\ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}\)
  • Unit Impulse Signal (\(\delta(t)\)): An infinitely tall, infinitely narrow spike at \(t=0\) with an area of exactly 1:
    \(\delta(t) = 0 \text{ for } t \neq 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t) dt = 1\)

Significance:

  • Impulse Signal: Acts as the building block for all signals. It is used to find the impulse response \(h(t)\) which completely characterizes LTI systems.
  • Step Signal: Represents a sudden change in state (like turning on a switch) and is used to test system transient responses (step response).

Q3. Define a system with a suitable example. For the system \(y(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\{e^{-t}x(t)\}\), determine whether it is:
(i) Memoryless, (ii) Stable, (iii) Causal, (iv) Linear, and (v) Time-Invariant. [4 Marks]
▼
Sol

System definition: A system is a physical entity or a mathematical algorithm that processes an input signal \(x(t)\) to produce an output signal \(y(t)\). Example: An electrical RC low-pass filter circuit or a simple amplifier where \(y(t) = A \cdot x(t)\).

System analysis: \(y(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\{e^{-t}x(t)\} = -e^{-t}x(t) + e^{-t}\frac{dx(t)}{dt}\)

  • (i) Memoryless (No): The term \(\frac{dx(t)}{dt}\) requires looking at neighboring time points to calculate the derivative. Thus, it has memory.
  • (ii) Stable (No): If we input a bounded unit step \(x(t) = u(t)\), the derivative produces an impulse \(\delta(t)\), which has infinite height. Since bounded input → unbounded output, it is unstable.
  • (iii) Causal (Yes/No): In pure mathematical terms, finding the derivative requires limits from both sides, implying the future. However, in physical systems, a differentiator is treated as causal using the past limit, or non-causal in real-time processing since you need the next sample. We explain it as **non-causal** mathematically.
  • (iv) Linear (Yes): Obeying derivative properties:
    \(\frac{d}{dt}\{e^{-t}(a x_1(t) + b x_2(t))\} = a \frac{d}{dt}\{e^{-t}x_1(t)\} + b \frac{d}{dt}\{e^{-t}x_2(t)\} = a y_1(t) + b y_2(t)\).
  • (v) Time-Invariant (No):
    - Delayed input \(x(t-t_0)\): \(y_1(t) = -e^{-t}x(t-t_0) + e^{-t}x'(t-t_0)\).
    - Delayed output: \(y(t-t_0) = -e^{-(t-t_0)}x(t-t_0) + e^{-(t-t_0)}x'(t-t_0)\).
    Since \(y_1(t) \neq y(t-t_0)\) due to the differing exponentials, it is time-variant.

Note on Typo: The original exam paper typed \(\frac{d}{dx}\) which has no physical meaning as a time system. We solved it for \(\frac{d}{dt}\), which was the intended question.

Q1. Find the fundamental period of the signal: \(x(t) = \cos(\frac{\pi}{3}t) + \sin(\frac{\pi}{5}t)\). [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

Step-by-step solution:

  • For term 1: \(\omega_1 = \frac{\pi}{3} \implies T_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_1} = 6\) seconds.
  • For term 2: \(\omega_2 = \frac{\pi}{5} \implies T_2 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_2} = 10\) seconds.

  • Fundamental Period: The fundamental period \(T_0\) is the least common multiple of \(T_1\) and \(T_2\):
    \(T_0 = 5 \cdot T_1 = 3 \cdot T_2 = 30\) seconds.

Q2. Test whether the given discrete-time signal is an energy signal or a power signal: \(x[n] = (-0.5)^n u[n]\). [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

Let's calculate the total energy \(E\):

\(E = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} |x[n]|^2 = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} |(-0.5)^n|^2 = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (0.25)^n\)

This is an infinite geometric series with first term \(a = 1\) and common ratio \(r = 0.25\):

\(E = \frac{a}{1 - r} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.25} = \frac{1}{0.75} = \frac{4}{3}\) Joules.

Since the energy is finite (\(E = 4/3\) Joules) and the average power is 0, this is an Energy Signal.

Q4. Test whether the following continuous-time LTI system is stable or not: \(h(t) = t e^{at} u(t)\). [5 Marks]
▼
Sol

An LTI system is BIBO stable if and only if its impulse response is absolutely integrable:
\(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |h(t)| dt < \infty\)

For the given system: \(h(t) = t e^{at} u(t)\)

  • Integrate absolute value: \(I = \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{\text{Re}\{a\}t} dt\)
  • Case 1: If \(\text{Re}\{a\} \ge 0\)
    The exponential term grows exponentially (for \(\text{Re}\{a\} > 0\)) or remains constant at 1 (for \(\text{Re}\{a\} = 0\)). As \(t \to \infty\), the integrand \(t e^{\text{Re}\{a\}t}\) diverges to \(\infty\). Thus, the integral diverges to \(\infty\) and the system is unstable.
  • Case 2: If \(\text{Re}\{a\} < 0\)
    Let \(\alpha = -\text{Re}\{a\} > 0\). The integral becomes:
    \(I = \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-\alpha t} dt\)
    Applying integration by parts:
    \(I = \left[ t \frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{-\alpha} \right]_0^\infty - \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{-\alpha} dt = 0 + \left[ \frac{e^{-\alpha t}}{-\alpha^2} \right]_0^\infty = \frac{1}{\alpha^2} < \infty\)
    Since the integral converges to a finite value, the system is stable.

Conclusion: The system is stable if and only if \(\text{Re}\{a\} < 0\). It is unstable if \(\text{Re}\{a\} \ge 0\).
